Sunflower

Helianthus annuus

Sunflowers start as seeds planted in warm, well-drained soil. The seed quickly sprouts a sturdy stem and broad leaves, then the plant grows tall — sometimes reaching over six feet — topped by a large flower head that tracks the sun when young. These plants need full sun to grow and flourish. After pollination, the flower head fills with hundreds of seeds and droops toward the ground as it ripens and dries.

Origins and History

Sunflowers are native to North America and were first cultivated by Indigenous peoples thousands of years ago for food, oil, and dye. Today, they grow in open fields, prairies, roadsides, and gardens across the world. They thrive in full sun with loose, well-drained soil and are especially common across the central and eastern United States.

Did You Know?

Sunflowers are used by people in many ways. Their seeds are eaten as snacks, pressed into cooking oil, and used in birdseed mixes. The tall, bright blooms are popular in cut flower arrangements and gardens. Sunflower oil is also found in soaps and cosmetics, and the plants have historically been used by Indigenous cultures for medicine and natural pigment. Additionally, sunflowers are sources of necter for bees and butterflies.
